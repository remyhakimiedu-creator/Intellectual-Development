# Intensity, Sensitivity, Giftedness and Neurodivergence: A Research Brief

**Scope:** Dabrowski's Theory of Positive Disintegration (TPD) and the five overexcitabilities (OEs), High Sensitivity (HSP / Sensory Processing Sensitivity), High Intellectual Potential (HIP / giftedness), ADHD, AuDHD and Twice-Exceptionality (2e), plus the social-media "that's me" effect.
**Purpose:** Background research for a multi-part content series.
**Date:** 14 Nov 2026

---

## 0. TL;DR (read this first)

- **Five frameworks, one shared vocabulary.** Dabrowski's OEs, Aron's HSP, gifted "intensity", ADHD and autism all describe the same *surface* traits: sensory overload, emotional intensity, justice sensitivity, deep focus, restlessness. They come from **different causes**, have **different evidence bases**, and have **different consequences**.
- **Only two of them are clinical diagnoses** (ADHD, autism). HSP and OEs are **temperament/personality constructs**. HIP/giftedness is an **ability classification**. 2e and AuDHD are **co-occurrence labels**, not separate diagnoses.
- **Viral checklists work because the items are non-specific.** Most listed traits are common, dimensional and found across many conditions, including anxiety, trauma, burnout and ordinary personality. That produces the Barnum effect *and* real recognition at the same time.
- **The line that matters is impairment plus developmental history plus how widespread the trait is, not intensity.** A trait becomes clinically relevant when it is lifelong, shows up across settings and costs you significantly. "I relate to this graphic" does not reach that bar.
- **The responsible angle for the series:** "These traits are real, and they're shared. Here's how to tell what might be driving yours, and when to get assessed."

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[Strong]** | Replicated, large samples, meta-analytic or consensus support |
| **[Moderate]** | Several peer-reviewed studies; some limitations |

## 1. Dabrowski's Theory of Positive Disintegration (TPD)

### 1.1 Who and what
- **Kazimierz Dąbrowski (1902–1980)**, Polish psychiatrist and psychologist. He developed TPD from the 1930s to the 1970s. The key English texts are *Positive Disintegration* (1964) and *Psychoneurosis Is Not an Illness* (1972).
- **His core claim:** advanced personality development often *requires* breaking apart a primitive, socially conforming psychological structure ("disintegration"). The person then rebuilds a higher, autonomous, values-driven one. On this view, anxiety, depression, inner conflict and existential crisis can be **developmental, not purely pathological**.
- **Evidence base:** clinical case work and biographical analysis of eminent people. It is **a philosophical-developmental theory, not an empirically derived model.** **[Weak/Contested as science; influential as a framework]**

### 1.2 The five levels (simplified)
| Level | Name | Gist |
|---|---|---|
| I | Primary Integration | Driven by biology and social conformity; little inner conflict |
| II | Unilevel Disintegration | Ambivalence and confusion with no clear "higher vs lower" direction |
| III | Spontaneous Multilevel Disintegration | Strong inner conflict between "what is" and "what ought to be" |
| IV | Organized Multilevel Disintegration | The person consciously steers their own development |
| V | Secondary Integration | Values-integrated, autonomous personality ("personality ideal") |

### 1.3 Developmental Potential (DP)
Dabrowski said a person's capacity for advanced development depends on three things:
1. **Overexcitabilities (OEs)**: heightened intensity of response to stimuli.
2. **Special talents and abilities.**
3. **The "Third Factor"**: an autonomous inner drive towards self-directed growth, separate from heredity (first factor) and environment (second factor).

**Key nuance for your series:** to Dabrowski, OEs were **raw material, not destiny**. High OE without the third factor could just mean a more painful life, not a "higher" one.

### 1.4 The five overexcitabilities
The Polish term is *nadpobudliwość psychiczna*, roughly "psychic superstimulability".

| OE | Core expression | Typical manifestations | Can be mistaken for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intellectual** | Drive to understand, analyse, question | Relentless "why?", theorising, avid reading, love of logic puzzles, intolerance of hand-wavy answers, moral/philosophical questioning | Autistic special interests; ADHD hyperfocus; "arrogance" |
| **Imaginational** | Vivid inner imagery and fantasy | Rich daydreams, imaginary worlds, metaphorical thinking, dramatisation, mixing fact and fiction, mind wandering in class | ADHD inattention; maladaptive daydreaming; dissociation |
| **Emotional** | Depth and intensity of feeling | Deep attachments, strong somatic reactions (stomach knots, flushing), empathy, complex mixed feelings, loneliness, strong memory for feelings, justice concern | Anxiety, depression, BPD-type dysregulation, ADHD emotional dysregulation |
| **Sensual** | Heightened sensory experience | Delight in or overload from sound, texture, taste, smell, light; aesthetic absorption; tags in clothes intolerable; sensory comfort-seeking | Autistic sensory reactivity; Sensory Processing Disorder; HSP |
| **Psychomotor** | Surplus physical energy | Rapid speech, restlessness, fidgeting, compulsive talking, need for movement, nervous habits/tics, workaholism | ADHD hyperactivity; anxiety; tic disorders |

### 1.5 The critical distinction: OEs are not a disorder
- OEs are **not in the DSM-5-TR or ICD-11**. They are not diagnoses, not a "condition", and there is **no clinical threshold**.
- They describe an **intensity profile**, a style of responding. That profile can be adaptive, costly or both, depending on the environment.
- Dabrowski deliberately **reframed** some distress as a sign of developmental potential. This is valuable (it reduces shame) and risky (it can delay treatment for real disorders).
- **What to say:** "Overexcitability describes *how intensely* you respond to the world. It doesn't explain *why*, and it doesn't rule out ADHD, autism, anxiety or trauma."

### 1.6 How OEs entered gifted education (the lineage)
- **Michael M. Piechowski**, Dabrowski's collaborator, brought OEs into gifted education from the late 1970s.
- **The Overexcitability Questionnaire (OEQ)** was developed by Lysy & Piechowski (1983). The **OEQ-II** (Falk, Lind, Miller, Piechowski & Silverman, 1999) has 50 self-report items, 10 per OE.
- **The Columbus Group (1991)** defined giftedness as *"asynchronous development… heightened intensity"*. This put intensity *inside* the definition of giftedness and is a major reason "gifted = intense = sensitive" spread.
- **Linda Silverman** and **James Webb** (*Misdiagnosis and Dual Diagnoses of Gifted Children and Adults*, 2005) popularised the argument that OEs are often **misdiagnosed** as ADHD, bipolar disorder and other conditions.

### 1.7 What the empirical research actually says about OEs
- **Gifted vs non-gifted:** a meta-analysis (Winkler & Voight, 2016, *Gifted Child Quarterly*) found gifted samples score higher on OEs. The difference is **clearest for Intellectual OE**, smaller for Imaginational, Emotional and Sensual, and negligible or inconsistent for Psychomotor. **[Moderate]**
- **Construct overlap:** Vuyk, Krieshok & Kerr (2016, *Gifted Child Quarterly*, "Openness to experience rather than overexcitabilities: Call it like it is") argued that OEs largely **overlap with the Big Five trait Openness to Experience**. Emotional OE also partly tracks **Neuroticism**. **[Moderate]**
  - *Implication:* OEs may be **existing personality traits under a new name**, not a unique profile of gifted people.
- **Measurement problems:** the evidence relies on self-report, mostly small or convenience samples, and has mixed psychometric support across translations. **[Weak/Contested]**
- **OE vs ADHD:** small studies comparing gifted students with and without ADHD exist, but they don't support using OE scores to "rule out" ADHD. **[Weak/Contested]**

**Bottom line:** OEs are a useful **descriptive language**. They are **not validated as distinct, measurable, gifted-specific traits**, and they **cannot differentiate** giftedness from ADHD or autism.

## 2. The Highly Sensitive Person (HSP) / Sensory Processing Sensitivity (SPS)

### 2.1 Origin
- **Elaine N. Aron & Arthur Aron (1997)**, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*: "Sensory-processing sensitivity and its relation to introversion and emotionality." This paper introduced the **27-item HSP Scale**. **[Strong as a founding paper]**
- Popular book: *The Highly Sensitive Person* (Aron, 1996).
- **Definition:** a temperament/personality trait involving **deeper cognitive processing of stimuli**, driven by heightened CNS sensitivity.

### 2.2 The "DOES" model (Aron)
- **D**epth of processing
- **O**verstimulation (easily overwhelmed)
- **E**motional reactivity and **E**mpathy
- **S**ensitivity to **S**ubtleties

### 2.3 Key research findings
- **Prevalence:** Aron's original estimate was ~15–20%. Lionetti et al. (2018, *Translational Psychiatry*, "Dandelions, tulips and orchids") found **three groups** using latent class analysis: low (~25–35%), medium (~40–47%) and high sensitivity (~20–35%). **[Moderate]**
  - *For your series:* roughly **1 in 3 to 1 in 5 people** fall in the "high" group. That is very common, which is one reason HSP posts get mass resonance.
- **Heritability:** a twin study (Assary et al., 2021, *Molecular Psychiatry*) estimated environmental sensitivity at **~47% heritable**. **[Moderate]**
- **Neural correlates:** fMRI studies (e.g., Acevedo et al., 2014, *Brain and Behavior*) link high SPS to greater activation in regions for awareness, empathy and integration (insula, inferior frontal gyrus, cingulate). These are **small samples**; treat them as suggestive. **[Weak–Moderate]**
- **Differential susceptibility:** this is the most important scientific frame. **Belsky & Pluess (2009)** and **Boyce & Ellis (2005)** proposed that sensitive people are affected **more by bad environments *and* benefit more from good ones** (the "orchid vs dandelion" idea). **[Moderate]**
- **Overlap with personality:** a meta-analysis (Lionetti et al., 2019, *Journal of Research in Personality*) found SPS correlates **positively with Neuroticism and Openness** and with negative affect. **[Moderate]**
- **Factor structure:** the HSP Scale tends to split into **Ease of Excitation, Low Sensory Threshold and Aesthetic Sensitivity** (Smolewska, McCabe & Woody, 2006). The first two relate to anxiety and distress; Aesthetic Sensitivity relates more to Openness. **[Moderate]**
- **Review of the field:** Greven et al. (2019, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) is the best single critical review to cite.

### 2.4 What HSP is not
- **Not a diagnosis.** It is not in the DSM or ICD.
- **Not the same as Sensory Processing Disorder** or autistic sensory hyper-reactivity, which involve **functional impairment**.
- **Not proof of giftedness.** HSP and IQ are separate dimensions.
- **Main critique:** the HSP Scale partly measures **general distress/neuroticism**. A high score can reflect current anxiety, burnout or trauma, not only an innate temperament. **[Moderate]**

## 3. High Intellectual Potential (HIP) / Giftedness

### 3.1 Terminology
- **HIP** comes from the French **Haut Potentiel Intellectuel (HPI)**. It is common in francophone and European discourse and is usually operationalised as **full-scale IQ ≥ 130**, two SDs above the mean. That is about **2.3% of the population**.
- **"Giftedness"** is broader in the Anglo-American literature:
  - **Marland Report (1972, US):** multiple domains (intellectual, creative, leadership, arts, academic).
  - **Renzulli's Three-Ring model:** above-average ability + creativity + task commitment.
  - **Gagné's DMGT:** *gifts* (natural potential) become *talents* (developed skill) through practice and environment.
  - **Columbus Group (1991):** asynchronous development and heightened intensity. This is the definition that bundles giftedness with sensitivity.

### 3.2 The "gifted = sensitive = suffering" narrative
- **Popular francophone literature** frames HPI as a whole personality profile: hypersensitivity, emotional intensity, feeling "different", existential angst. Jeanne Siaud-Facchin's *Trop intelligent pour être heureux?* (2008) and the **"zèbre"** metaphor are the main examples. **[Popular]**
- **Evidence pushback:**
  - Martin, Burns & Schonlau (2010, *Gifted Child Quarterly*) reviewed epidemiological studies. They found gifted youth have **similar or lower** rates of most mental-health problems than peers. **[Moderate]**
  - Francophone researchers **Nicolas Gauvrit and Franck Ramus** have argued against the "dark legend" of the gifted, meaning the idea that high IQ predicts emotional fragility. **[Moderate, academic critique]**
  - Karpinski et al. (2018, *Intelligence*), "High intelligence: A risk factor for psychological and physiological overexcitabilities", reported higher rates of mood, anxiety and other disorders in **Mensa members**. It is often cited, and heavily criticised for **self-selection and self-report bias**. **[Weak/Contested]**
- **Fair synthesis:** high IQ is **not** reliably linked to more emotional fragility at the population level. However, **specific subgroups** do face real friction: gifted people in poorly fitting environments, those with asynchronous development, and 2e individuals.

### 3.3 Key point for the series
IQ is **measurable**. Sensitivity and intensity are **not the same construct**. "I'm sensitive and I overthink, so I must be HIP" is a **logical error**: it affirms the consequent using non-specific traits.

## 4. The Convergence Zone: ADHD, Autism, AuDHD and 2e

### 4.1 Clinical anchors (the conditions that *are* diagnoses)
| Condition | Core diagnostic features (DSM-5-TR) | Approx. prevalence | Heritability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ADHD** | Persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity; several symptoms **before age 12**; in **2+ settings**; clear **impairment** | ~5–7% of children; ~2.5–3% persistent in adults (higher with broader criteria) **[Strong]** | ~70–80% (Faraone & Larsson, 2019) **[Strong]** |
| **Autism (ASD)** | Persistent **social-communication differences** + **restricted/repetitive behaviours and interests**, including **sensory hyper/hypo-reactivity (criterion B4)**; present from early development; impairment | US CDC: ~1 in 31 eight-year-olds (2022 surveillance data) **[Strong]** | ~80% (Sandin et al., 2017; Bai et al., 2019) **[Strong]** |

**Not in the diagnostic criteria, but very common:**
- **ADHD:** **emotional dysregulation** (Shaw et al., 2014, *American Journal of Psychiatry*). Prominent in a large share of people with ADHD. **[Strong]**
- **Autism:** **sensory differences** in most autistic people, with many studies reporting 70–90%+ (e.g., Ben-Sasson et al., 2009 meta-analysis). **[Strong]**

### 4.2 AuDHD (Autism + ADHD)
- Before **DSM-5 (2013)**, autism and ADHD could not be diagnosed together. Now they can.
- **Co-occurrence:** a meta-analysis (Rong et al., 2021) estimated **~40%** of autistic people have ADHD; individual studies range from ~30% to 70%. **[Strong for "very common"; the exact figure varies]**
- **"AuDHD" is a community term, not a formal diagnosis.** Clinically it is two diagnoses.
- **Phenotype features often described:**
  - A tug-of-war between **novelty-seeking (ADHD) and routine-needing (autism)**.
  - **Sensory sensitivity** from both sides.
  - **Emotional intensity** and **justice/fairness sensitivity**.
  - Masking/camouflaging, especially in women and girls, which leads to **late diagnosis**.
  - **[Moderate for co-occurrence; Weak–Moderate for the specific "AuDHD profile" descriptions, which are largely clinical observation and lived experience]**

### 4.3 Twice-Exceptionality (2e)
- **Definition:** a person who is **both** gifted **and** has one or more disabilities (ADHD, autism, a specific learning disorder such as dyslexia, anxiety and others). The most-cited operational definition is **Reis, Baum & Burke (2014, *Gifted Child Quarterly*)**.
- **The "masking" model** (widely used in 2e literature) **[Moderate as a clinical framework]**:
  1. **Giftedness masks disability.** High ability compensates, so the person is "bright but underperforming" and gets diagnosed late.
  2. **Disability masks giftedness.** Struggles hide potential, so the person is never identified as gifted.
  3. **Each masks the other.** The person appears "average" and neither need is met.
- **Is ADHD "real" in high-IQ people, or just OE?** Antshel et al. (2007, *Psychological Medicine*) found that high-IQ children with ADHD show **similar impairment, family patterns and comorbidity** to typical-IQ ADHD. The diagnosis is **valid** in gifted people. **[Moderate–Strong]**
  - This directly counters the strong form of the "it's just overexcitability, not ADHD" argument.
- **Assessment caveat:** cognitive testing in 2e people often shows a **large gap between strong verbal/reasoning scores and weaker working-memory/processing-speed scores**. That gap can drag the full-scale IQ down and hide giftedness. **[Moderate]**

### 4.4 Overlap map: one trait, many possible sources
This is the most important table for your series.

| Viral trait | HSP/SPS | OE (Dabrowski) | Giftedness | ADHD | Autism | Anxiety / Trauma / Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensory overload** (noise, lights, textures) | Core (low sensory threshold) | Sensual OE | Not inherent | Common (filtering difficulty) | Core (criterion B4) | Anxiety, PTSD hypervigilance, migraine, sleep deprivation |
| **Emotional intensity** | Core | Emotional OE | Not inherent | Very common (emotional dysregulation) | Common (meltdowns, alexithymia) | Depression, BPD, CPTSD, bipolar spectrum |
| **Justice / fairness sensitivity** | Plausible via empathy | Emotional/Intellectual OE | Often reported | Elevated "victim sensitivity" (Bondü & Esser, 2015) **[Moderate]** | Often reported; small studies **[Weak]** | Moral development, trauma, cultural values |
| **Perfectionism** | Linked via conscientious processing | Not core | Common, mixed adaptive/maladaptive | Often compensatory | Rigidity / "rules must be right" | Anxiety, OCD/OCPD, high-pressure parenting |
| **Overthinking / rumination** | Depth of processing | Intellectual OE | Linked | Racing thoughts | Repetitive thinking | Generalised anxiety, depression |
| **Hyperfocus / intense interests** | Not core | Intellectual OE | Common | Hyperfocus on high-interest tasks | Core (restricted interests) | Mania (rare), avoidance coping |
| **Restlessness / fidgeting / fast speech** | Not core | Psychomotor OE | Not inherent | Core | Stimming (self-regulation) | Anxiety, hyperthyroidism, caffeine, stimulants |
| **Feeling "different" / outsider** | Common | Linked | Common | Common | Common | Social anxiety, cultural mismatch, bullying history, ordinary adolescence |

**Takeaway:** **every item on a typical viral checklist has 4–7 plausible explanations.** A checklist that lists only these traits **cannot discriminate between conditions.** That isn't a flaw of one post. It is how these traits work: they are **transdiagnostic**.

### 4.5 What actually discriminates (clinical logic)
These are the questions that separate trait from condition and one condition from another:
1. **Developmental history.** Present since early childhood (ADHD, autism, temperament), or started after a life event (trauma, depression, burnout)?
2. **How widespread it is.** In all settings (home, work, social), or only in specific stressful contexts?
3. **Impairment.** Does it clearly reduce functioning (grades, job, relationships, health)? Or is it intense but manageable?
4. **The core features, not the shared ones.**
   - ADHD: **executive dysfunction** (time blindness, task initiation, working memory, losing things) persisting even when motivated.
   - Autism: **social-communication differences** and **restricted/repetitive patterns**, not just sensitivity.
   - Giftedness: **measured cognitive ability**.
   - HSP: **depth of processing + overstimulation + benefits strongly from supportive environments**, *without* impairment.
5. **Response to environment.** Does the trait shrink a lot with sleep, less stress and a good fit (temperament/SPS, situational anxiety)? Or does it persist regardless (neurodevelopmental)?

## 5. Why These Lists Go Viral: Psychology of the "That's Me" Effect

### 5.1 The Barnum / Forer effect
- **Forer (1949)**, "The fallacy of personal validation". Students rated a generic personality description, which was in fact identical for everyone, as highly accurate for themselves (mean ≈ 4.3/5).
- **Meehl (1956)** coined the term **"Barnum effect"**.
- **Conditions that strengthen it**, all present in viral graphics:
  - **Vague, double-sided statements** ("You're sensitive but hide it well").
  - **Positive or flattering framing** ("deep thinker", "old soul").
  - **Perceived authority**: aesthetic graphics, clinical-sounding terms.
  - **Personalisation**: "If you do these things, you might be…"

### 5.2 Other mechanisms at play
| Mechanism | How it works on Threads/TikTok |
|---|---|
| **High base rates** | Traits like overthinking, sensory annoyance and fairness concern are present in a **majority** of people to some degree, so resonance is guaranteed |
| **Dimensional traits read as categories** | Everyone has *some* sensitivity; a list implies a yes/no category |
| **Confirmation bias** | People recall moments that fit ("I hated tags as a kid!") and ignore those that don't |
| **Identity and belonging** | A label gives community, language and self-compassion. It is emotionally rewarding, so people adopt it more readily |
| **Explanatory relief** | For people with lifelong unexplained struggles, a label feels like an answer. This is **sometimes accurate**, which strengthens the whole pattern |
| **Algorithmic amplification** | "This is so me 😭" comments and shares drive reach, so the most broadly relatable (least specific) content wins |
| **Flattery bundling** | Struggles (overwhelm) packaged with virtues (empathy, intelligence, depth) feel validating, not pathologising |
| **Concept creep** (Haslam, 2016) | Clinical concepts expand to cover milder, everyday experiences |
| **Prevalence inflation** (Foulkes & Andrews, 2023) | Greater awareness can lead people to interpret normal distress as disorder, which may increase real symptoms |

### 5.3 What the research says about social media content quality
- **ADHD on TikTok:** Yeung, Ng & Abi-Jaoude (2022, *Canadian Journal of Psychiatry*) rated **~52% of the top 100 ADHD videos as misleading**. **[Moderate]**
- **Autism on TikTok:** Aragon-Guevara et al. (2023, *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*) found a substantial share of popular videos **inaccurate or overgeneralised**. **[Moderate]**
- **Social contagion example:** during 2020–2021, clinicians reported a surge of **functional tic-like behaviours**, mainly in adolescent girls, linked to exposure to TikTok tic content. Some researchers called it **"mass social media-induced illness"** (Müller-Vahl et al., 2022). **[Moderate]** This is the strongest real-world example that online symptom exposure can shape symptom presentation.

### 5.4 The other side: genuine recognition is real
Don't swing into dismissal. Valid reasons people recognise themselves:
- **Under-diagnosis:** many adults, especially **women, girls and minority groups**, were missed as children because of masking, stereotypes and limited access to services. **[Strong]**
- **Late-diagnosed adults often say social media was their first prompt** to seek assessment, and some are then clinically confirmed.
- Validated self-report screeners (e.g., **ASRS v1.1** for adult ADHD; **AQ** for autistic traits) are **legitimate first steps**. But they are **screeners, not diagnoses**: high false-positive rates, especially when anxiety or depression is present.

**Balanced framing:** *"Social media is a reasonable place to start asking questions and a bad place to finish answering them."*

## 6. The Danger of Self-Labeling from a Single Graphic

### 6.1 Risks
1. **Missed or wrong diagnosis.** Attention problems from **depression, anxiety, sleep deprivation, thyroid issues, substance use or trauma** get labelled as ADHD or "giftedness" and go untreated.
2. **Delayed treatment.** "It's just my overexcitability" can block help for treatable conditions. The reverse also happens: "it's ADHD" can block trauma work.
3. **Identity lock-in.** A label can become an explanation for everything ("I can't do X because I'm HSP"). That reduces the sense of agency and the willingness to try things that would help.
4. **Self-fulfilling expectations.** Expecting to be overwhelmed can **increase** attention to and reporting of overwhelm (a nocebo-like effect; also part of the prevalence-inflation argument).
5. **Status framing.** "Gifted" and "highly sensitive" carry implicit **superiority**: deeper, more empathic, more intelligent. That can reinforce elitism and isolation.
6. **Dilution harms people with real impairment.** When "everyone is a bit ADHD/autistic", people with significant disability are taken less seriously.

### 6.2 The trauma confound (important and under-discussed)
**Complex trauma and chronic stress** can produce a profile almost identical to the viral checklist: hypervigilance (sensory sensitivity), emotional intensity, people-pleasing, perfectionism, justice sensitivity, concentration problems and feeling different.
- ICD-11 **Complex PTSD** adds "disturbances in self-organisation": affect dysregulation, negative self-concept and relational difficulties.
- **Key differentiator:** **onset and context.** Trauma-related patterns often start or intensify after adversity and are triggered by specific cues. Neurodevelopmental traits are present from early childhood across contexts.
- They can **co-occur**. Neurodivergent people experience higher rates of bullying and adverse experiences.

### 6.3 A responsible framing model: three layers
Use this as a recurring visual in the series:

```
LAYER 1: Human variation
  Everyone has some sensitivity, intensity, curiosity, restlessness.
  → Normal. No label needed.

LAYER 2: Temperament / ability profiles (HSP, OEs, giftedness)
  Stronger-than-average traits; shape preferences and needs;
  NOT disorders; no impairment required.
  → Self-knowledge, environment design.

LAYER 3: Clinical conditions (ADHD, autism, anxiety, PTSD...)
  Persistent, pervasive, developmentally rooted (or event-triggered),
  and IMPAIRING.
  → Professional assessment and support.

Overlay: Trauma / stress / burnout can mimic Layers 2 and 3.
```

### 6.4 A three-question filter for your audience
1. **Since when?** Since childhood, or since a particular period or event?
2. **Where?** Everywhere, or only in specific situations?
3. **What does it cost?** Is it interfering with work, study, relationships or health, consistently?

If the answers are *childhood + everywhere + significant cost*, recommend **professional assessment** (a clinical psychologist or psychiatrist). If not, it is probably **temperament or context**, and practical adjustments may be enough.

### Tone and framing rules
- **Do:** validate experiences, explain mechanisms, give base rates, name the evidence quality, point towards assessment.
- **Don't:** use "signs you're secretly…" hooks without debunking them, imply superiority, diagnose viewers, or dismiss self-identification wholesale.
- **Recurring disclaimer** (short, not preachy):
  > *"This is education, not diagnosis. If these traits are costing you at work, school or in relationships, a qualified clinician can help you sort out what's driving them."*

### Sharp, accurate lines you can reuse
- "Intensity tells you *how much*. It doesn't tell you *why*."
- "A trait shared by 30% of people is not a secret code."
- "ADHD isn't 'being distracted'. It's being unable to *not* be distracted, even when it matters and even when you care."
- "Sensitivity can be temperament, neurodivergence or a nervous system that learned to stay on guard. Same feeling, different fix."
- "Social media is a good place to start asking questions and a bad place to finish answering them."

## 9. Known Uncertainties and Open Debates

- **Are OEs a distinct construct, or just Openness plus Neuroticism?** Unresolved; the evidence leans towards substantial overlap.
- **Is SPS a distinct trait, or a blend of neuroticism, openness and sensory threshold?** Active debate; the differential-susceptibility framing is the most defensible.
- **Do gifted people have a distinct emotional profile?** Population data mostly say no; clinical and advocacy literature says yes for subgroups. Sampling differences explain much of the gap.
- **"AuDHD" as its own phenotype:** a widely used community concept; research on a distinct combined profile is still developing.
- **Prevalence inflation vs under-diagnosis:** **both are happening at once**, in different populations. This is the most intellectually honest position to take publicly.
