# Threads: Interesting Findings on Intelligence, Curiosity and the Gifted Brain

Each numbered post is ≤500 characters (the Threads limit). Sources are listed at the bottom.

## THREAD 1: Why can high-IQ people struggle academically?

**1/**
IQ predicts grades. But not as much as you'd think.

A meta-analysis of 240 samples found IQ correlates with school grades at about r = 0.54.

That sounds strong. But it means IQ explains only ~30% of the differences in grades.

The other 70% is something else. 🧵

**2/**
Here's part of that "something else":

In a study of 8th graders, self-discipline predicted final grades better than IQ did. (Duckworth & Seligman, 2005)

Being smart gets you in the room. Self-discipline decides what you do once you're there.

**3/**
The "gifted kid" trap:

If school was easy for you, you never had to build a study system. Then one day, at university or in a hard subject, raw intelligence stops being enough.

You aren't getting dumber. You're meeting difficulty for the first time, without tools.

**4/**
It gets worse if you were praised for being "smart."

In a classic study, children praised for intelligence ("you're so smart") later chose easier tasks and performed worse after failing than kids praised for effort. (Mueller & Dweck, 1998)

"Smart" becomes an identity to protect, not a skill to grow.

**5/**
And sometimes the struggle isn't motivation at all.

High IQ can hide ADHD, dyslexia or other learning difficulties. Ability compensates, so the person looks "lazy but bright" for years.

Psychologists call this being twice-exceptional (2e).

**6/**
The takeaway:

Intelligence is the engine. Study skills, self-regulation and the right environment are the steering wheel.

A powerful engine with no steering doesn't go further. It just crashes faster.

## THREAD 2: Intellectual curiosity vs ADHD hyperfocus: what's the difference?

**1/**
"I get lost in a topic for 6 hours straight."

That could be intellectual curiosity, or it could be ADHD hyperfocus.

They look identical from outside. Inside, they work completely differently. 🧵

**2/**
Intellectual overexcitability (from the psychiatrist Kazimierz Dąbrowski) is a drive to understand: questioning, theorising, reading deeply.

The key word is DRIVE. You go deep because you want to understand.

**3/**
ADHD hyperfocus is about REGULATION, not curiosity.

The ADHD brain struggles to direct attention on command. Attention gets pulled by interest, novelty and urgency, then gets stuck there.

Adults with ADHD report hyperfocus more often than adults without it. (Hupfeld et al., 2019)

**4/**
The test that separates them:

Can you switch to the boring-but-important task when you need to?

• Curious person: "I'd rather keep reading, but fine, I'll do my tax forms."
• ADHD: "I KNOW the forms matter. I care. I still can't make my brain start."

**5/**
Another clue: what happens afterwards.

Deep focus driven by curiosity usually feels satisfying, like flow.

ADHD hyperfocus often ends with "Wait, it's 3am, I didn't eat, and I missed the deadline I actually cared about."

**6/**
And yes, a person can have both.

A curious mind with ADHD isn't a contradiction. It's one of the most common profiles among twice-exceptional adults.

The question isn't "Do I hyperfocus?" It's "Do I control where my focus goes?"

## THREAD 3: Why are some people naturally drawn to philosophy, science, politics and history?

**1/**
Some people read about history for fun. Some argue philosophy at 2am. Some can't stop following politics.

Psychology has a name for this, and it isn't just "being smart." 🧵

**2/**
It's called Openness to Experience, specifically the "Intellect" side of it.

It's one of the Big Five personality traits, and it predicts interest in ideas, abstraction and complexity, more than IQ alone does.

**3/**
A related trait: Need for Cognition (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982).

Some people genuinely ENJOY effortful thinking. For them, a hard question isn't a burden. It's entertainment.

That's why some people choose documentaries over Netflix dramas, without trying to look smart.

**4/**
Why does curiosity feel so good?

A brain-imaging study found that curiosity activates the brain's dopamine reward circuit, and it boosted memory even for unrelated information learned at the same time. (Gruber et al., 2014)

Curious brains are literally rewarded for learning.

**5/**
Behavioural economist George Loewenstein called it the "information gap": curiosity is the discomfort of knowing that you don't know.

Philosophy, science and history are endless information gaps. For some brains, that's irresistible.

**6/**
Why those subjects especially?

• Philosophy: "why" questions with no final answer
• Science: systems and patterns
• History: cause and effect at human scale
• Politics: justice, power and fairness

Each one rewards a different flavour of the same itch: needing the world to make sense.

## THREAD 4: Can giftedness coexist with ADHD and executive dysfunction?

**1/**
"You can't have ADHD, you're too smart."

This is one of the most damaging myths in psychology, and the research directly contradicts it. 🧵

**2/**
A study at Massachusetts General Hospital compared high-IQ adults (IQ ≥120) with and without ADHD.

The high-IQ ADHD group had:
• lower quality of life
• more work and family problems
• more accidents and arrests
• more depression and anxiety

Exactly the same pattern as average-IQ ADHD. (Antshel et al., 2009)

**3/**
Why does that happen? Because intelligence and executive function are different systems.

IQ = how well you reason and solve problems.
Executive function = whether you can start, plan, organise, switch and finish.

You can have a Ferrari engine with bicycle brakes.

**4/**
Another finding from the same group: high-IQ adults with ADHD scored LOWER on executive-function tests than other high-IQ adults, but still in the "average" range overall. (Antshel et al., 2010)

So they looked "fine" on paper. That's exactly why they get missed.

**5/**
Psychologists call this "masking":

• Giftedness hides the disability → "bright but lazy"
• The disability hides the giftedness → "just an average student"
• Both cancel out → no one notices either

**6/**
If you've always been called "so much potential, if only you'd apply yourself," it isn't a diagnosis. But it might be worth a proper assessment.

Potential that keeps not turning into output is information, not a character flaw.

## THREAD 5: Is curiosity a better predictor of lifelong learning than grades?

**1/**
Grades get you through the door. Curiosity decides whether you keep learning after the door closes.

What the research says about the "hungry mind": 🧵

**2/**
Researchers von Stumm, Hell & Chamorro-Premuzic (2011) called intellectual curiosity "the third pillar of academic performance."

Their meta-analysis found curiosity and effort together predicted performance about as strongly as intelligence did.

**3/**
Psychologist Raymond Cattell's "investment theory":

Raw reasoning ability turns into real knowledge only when you INVEST it, through interests and engagement.

Two people with the same IQ, 20 years later: the curious one knows far more.

**4/**
In adults, "typical intellectual engagement" (how much you seek out thinking in daily life) predicts how much knowledge people accumulate across their lives. (Ackerman and colleagues)

School tests what you learned. Curiosity predicts what you'll keep learning.

**5/**
Fair point though: grades still matter.

They predict which doors open (scholarships, jobs, programmes) because they measure conscientiousness as well as knowledge.

So it's not grades OR curiosity. Grades are the short-term signal. Curiosity is the long-term engine.

**6/**
If you finished school with average grades but you still read, question and explore, you didn't peak. You're compounding.

## THREAD 6 (bonus): Why smart people still fall for bad arguments

**1/**
Intelligence doesn't protect you from irrational thinking. In some cases, it makes it worse. 🧵

**2/**
Psychologist Keith Stanovich coined "dysrationalia": the gap between intelligence and rational thinking.

His research found that "myside bias" (judging evidence by whether it supports your side) is largely unrelated to IQ.

**3/**
Even more striking, Dan Kahan's research:

When data was politically neutral, people with stronger maths skills interpreted it more accurately.

When the SAME data touched a political issue, the strongest maths people became MORE polarised, using their skill to reach the answer they already wanted.

**4/**
Why? Intelligence is a tool. It can search for truth, or build better defences for what you already believe.

A smarter person is often just a better lawyer for their own opinions.

**5/**
What protects you isn't IQ. It's intellectual humility: the willingness to ask "What would change my mind?"

Real intelligence includes the ability to lose an argument and grow from it.

## THREAD 7 (bonus): Why smart people procrastinate

**1/**
"If you're so smart, why do you procrastinate?"

Because procrastination was never an intelligence problem. 🧵

**2/**
A major meta-analysis (Steel, 2007) found procrastination has little to no link with intelligence.

What it IS linked to: impulsiveness, low conscientiousness, and how unpleasant the task feels.

**3/**
Researchers Sirois & Pychyl describe procrastination as an emotion-regulation problem, not a time-management problem.

You aren't avoiding the task. You're avoiding the feeling the task gives you: boredom, anxiety, fear of not being good enough.

**4/**
Smart people have an extra trap: perfectionism.

If your identity is "the smart one," starting a task risks proving you aren't. Not starting protects the image.

"I could have done it well, I just didn't try" feels safer than "I tried and it was average."

**5/**
The fix isn't more willpower. It's lowering the emotional cost of starting:

• make the first step stupidly small
• aim for a bad first draft
• decide in advance when you'll start

Start ugly. Improve later.
